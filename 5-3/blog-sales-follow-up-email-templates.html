The Sales Follow-Up Email: 7 Templates That Actually Get Replies

Andrew BryceFounder & CEO, Sixty Seconds·16 March 2026·12 min read
Quick Answer

The best sales follow-up emails reference something specific from the conversation, include one clear next action, and arrive within 4 hours of the meeting. Below are 7 copy-paste templates for every common sales scenario.

You had a great meeting. The buyer was engaged, asked smart questions, maybe even hinted at budget. You walk back to your desk. Three Slack messages. Two fires. A missed call.

The follow-up email sits in your mental to-do list. By the next morning, the details have blurred. So you write something generic: "Great chatting today, let me know if you have any questions." The buyer reads it, thinks "this person wasn't paying attention", and moves on.

This is how most deals die. Not in the negotiation. In the silence after the first meeting.

Why Most Follow-Up Emails Fail

The problem isn't laziness. It's timing and specificity.

Research from Yesware found that follow-ups sent within the first hour after a meeting get a 45% response rate. After 24 hours, that drops to 18%. After 48 hours, you're competing with every other email in their inbox, and you've lost your advantage: the conversation is still fresh in both your minds.

But speed alone doesn't fix it. A fast, generic email is just fast noise. The real difference is specificity: referencing something the buyer actually said, connecting it to a concrete next step, and making it easy to reply.

Specific reference + clear next action + easy reply = follow-up that moves the deal.

Every template below follows this formula. Copy them, adapt the brackets, and send.

How to Use These Templates

Each template targets a specific scenario you'll hit after a sales meeting. Pick the one that matches, swap out the bracketed sections with your real details, and send within 4 hours of the meeting.

Two rules:

  • Personalise the first line. The bracketed [specific thing they said] is the most important part. This is what separates "I was listening" from "I send this to everyone."
  • Keep it short. Every template below is under 120 words. Your follow-up is not a proposal. It's a bridge to the next conversation.

Template 1: After a First Discovery Call

Use when: You've just had an introductory meeting and want to confirm mutual interest and lock in a next step.

Subject: [Their pain point] + next step

Hi [Name],

Good speaking with you earlier. When you mentioned [specific challenge they described], it resonated because we see that a lot with [similar company type/industry].

I've put together a brief overview of how we've helped [similar company or use case] tackle that same problem. Attached / linked below.

Would [specific day] at [specific time] work for a follow-up to walk through it? Happy to adjust if another time suits better.

Cheers,
[Your name]

Why this works: Opens with their words, not yours. Positions you as someone who listened, not someone running through a pitch checklist. The attached resource gives them something tangible, and the specific time suggestion removes friction from scheduling.

Common mistake: Writing "It was great to meet you" as the opening line. That tells them nothing. Lead with what they told you.

Template 2: After a Demo or Product Walkthrough

Use when: They've seen the product and you need to move toward a decision or next step.

Subject: [Product/feature they reacted to] + your [timeline/goal]

Hi [Name],

Thanks for the time today. I noticed you spent the most time asking about [specific feature or workflow they focused on]. That tells me [inference about their priority].

Based on what you shared about [their goal or deadline], here's what I'd suggest as a next step:

[One clear action: trial setup, proposal, intro to decision-maker, or technical review]

I can have that ready by [date]. Does that timeline work on your end?

[Your name]

Why this works: You're showing you noticed what they cared about most, not just running through your demo script. The inference ("that tells me...") proves you're thinking about their problem, not your product. The proactive next step with a date removes the "what happens now?" ambiguity that kills momentum.

Common mistake: Recapping every feature you showed. They were there. They don't need a transcript. Focus on the one thing that landed.

Template 3: After a Meeting Where Budget Was Discussed

Use when: Money came up. Pricing, budget constraints, ROI expectations. This is a high-intent signal. Don't waste it.

Subject: [Their company] + [your product] pricing clarity

Hi [Name],

Following up on our conversation, particularly around the budget side.

You mentioned [specific budget context: "needing to stay under £X", "getting approval from finance", "comparing against current spend"]. That's helpful because it lets me tailor what I send over.

I've put together [a proposal / pricing breakdown / ROI comparison] based on those numbers. See attached.

Two things to note:
1. [Key financial point that addresses their concern]
2. [Flexible option: payment terms, tier, or pilot]

Worth a 15-minute call on [day] to walk through the numbers together?

[Your name]

Why this works: Budget conversations are trust signals. The buyer opened up about money. Your follow-up should honour that by being direct, not dancing around pricing with "let's schedule a call to discuss." You've already discussed it. Now you deliver clarity.

Common mistake: Ignoring the budget conversation and sending a generic follow-up. If they raised budget, your very next email should address it head-on.

Template 4: When They've Gone Quiet (The Nudge)

Use when: It's been 5-7 days since your last touchpoint and they haven't responded. Not a chase email. A value-add nudge.

Subject: [Something relevant to their situation]

Hi [Name],

I came across [relevant article / case study / data point that connects to their challenge] and thought of our conversation about [specific topic].

[One-sentence summary of why it's relevant to them.]

No pressure on next steps. Just wanted to share something useful. If timing has shifted on your end, totally understand. Happy to pick things back up whenever it makes sense.

[Your name]

Why this works: This is the anti-"just checking in" email. Instead of asking for something, you're giving something. The relevant resource proves you're still thinking about their problem, not just your pipeline. And "no pressure" with "happy to pick things back up" keeps the door open without being pushy.

Common mistake: Writing "Just checking in to see if you've had a chance to review..." That email is about you, not them. Always lead with value.

Template 5: After a Multi-Stakeholder Meeting

Use when: More than one person from the buyer's side was in the meeting. You need to align everyone and surface the real decision-maker's concerns.

Subject: Summary + next steps from [meeting date]

Hi [Name],

Great session with the wider team today. Quick summary of what we covered so everyone's aligned:

Key discussion points:
- [Point 1 that was raised, attributed to who raised it]
- [Point 2]
- [Point 3]

Open questions:
- [Question that wasn't fully resolved]

Agreed next step:
- [Specific action + owner + date]

I'll have [deliverable] ready by [date]. If I've missed anything or anyone wants to add context, just reply to this thread.

[Your name]

Why this works: Multi-stakeholder deals stall because nobody writes down what was agreed. This email becomes the source of truth. Attributing points to specific people shows you were tracking the room, not just presenting. The "agreed next step" with an owner and date creates accountability.

Common mistake: Sending this to only your main contact. CC everyone who was in the room. The summary email is your chance to stay visible to the full buying committee.

Template 6: When You Need to Re-Engage a Dead Deal

Use when: The deal went cold 30-90 days ago. Something has changed (new feature, new case study, their company news) that gives you a reason to reach out.

Subject: [Trigger event or change that's relevant to them]

Hi [Name],

It's been a while since we last spoke. I noticed [trigger: their company raised funding / hired for a role / launched a new product / their competitor did something relevant].

When we last chatted, [specific challenge they had] was the main sticking point. Since then, [what's changed on your side: new feature, customer result, pricing change].

Worth a quick 15 minutes to see if the timing's better now? No worries if not. Just didn't want you to miss something that might be relevant.

[Your name]

Why this works: Cold outreach to a warm contact is a different game. You have history. Referencing your previous conversation and pairing it with a genuine trigger event (not "just reaching out") gives them a reason to re-engage.

Common mistake: Sending "Hope you're doing well! Just wanted to circle back..." Circle back to what? If you can't name a specific trigger for why you're reaching out now, wait until you can.

Template 7: The AI-Assisted Follow-Up

Use when: You want the specificity of templates 1-6 but don't want to spend 15 minutes writing each one manually.

The hardest part of every template above is the bracketed sections. The [specific thing they said]. The [challenge they described]. The [feature they reacted to]. Pulling those details from a 45-minute conversation is what takes time, and it's why most reps default to generic emails instead.

This is where AI meeting tools earn their keep. Tools like 60 analyse your meeting recording, extract the key moments (what the buyer cared about, what objections came up, what was agreed), and draft a follow-up that references those specifics. The rep reviews it, adjusts the tone, and sends. Total time: about 60 seconds instead of 15 minutes.

Whether you use AI or do this manually, the principle stays the same: specificity wins. A personalised follow-up sent 4 hours later outperforms a generic one sent in 10 minutes.

The AI-assisted version of Template 1 looks like this:

Hi [Name],

Good call earlier. When you mentioned that your team is spending 3 hours a day on manual CRM updates and it's pulling reps away from actual selling, that stood out. We hear that from a lot of [their company size] sales teams.

I've attached a one-pager showing how [similar customer] cut that admin time by 70% in the first month.

Does Thursday at 2pm work for a follow-up? Happy to adjust.

Cheers,
[Your name]

Notice the difference: "spending 3 hours a day on manual CRM updates" is a direct reference to something the buyer said. That's the detail AI pulls from the meeting transcript. That's what makes the buyer think "they were actually listening."

The Timing Rules

When you send matters almost as much as what you send.

  • First meeting follow-up: within 4 hours. Details are fresh. You beat 90% of reps who wait until tomorrow.
  • Post-demo: within 2 hours. Momentum from seeing the product is highest right after the demo.
  • Budget discussion: same day. Budget conversations create urgency. Don't let it cool.
  • Multi-stakeholder: within 2 hours. Be the person who writes the summary before anyone else does.
  • Nudge (gone quiet): day 5-7. Enough time to not seem desperate. Soon enough to still be relevant.
  • Re-engage dead deal: when the trigger happens. Not on a schedule. Only when you have a real reason.

The pattern: faster is better for everything except nudges and re-engagement, where timing needs to be driven by relevance, not urgency.

5 Follow-Up Mistakes That Kill Deals

1. Starting with "I"
"I wanted to follow up..." "I just wanted to..." "I hope you're well..."
Every one of these makes the email about you. Start with what they said, what happened, or what you're sending them.

2. No specific next action
"Let me know if you'd like to chat" is not a next action. "Does Thursday at 2pm work for a 15-minute call to review the proposal?" is. Give them something specific to say yes to.

3. Attaching too much
One resource. One link. One thing to look at. Not a pitch deck, a case study, a pricing sheet, and a product overview. You're creating homework, not momentum.

4. The "per my last email" chase
If they haven't replied, sending the same email again with "just bumping this up" tells them you have nothing new to offer. Every follow-up must add something: new information, a relevant resource, or a different angle.

5. Waiting too long
Every day you wait, your follow-up competes with more noise. The buyer's memory of your conversation fades. Their priorities shift. Speed and specificity together are the competitive advantage most reps ignore.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many follow-up emails should you send before giving up?

Three to four touchpoints total, spread across 2-3 weeks. After that, shift to a long-term nurture approach (sharing relevant content every 4-6 weeks) rather than direct follow-ups. Persistence is good. Pestering is not.

Should you follow up on LinkedIn or email?

Email for the primary follow-up (it's where business communication lives). LinkedIn as a secondary touchpoint: like their recent post, comment on something relevant, or send a brief message that references your email. Using both channels increases visibility without doubling the pressure.

What's the best subject line for a follow-up email?

Reference something specific from the meeting. "[Their company] + [topic you discussed]" outperforms generic subjects like "Following up" or "Great meeting today." The subject line should tell them exactly why they should open the email.

How long should a follow-up email be?

Under 150 words. Ideally under 120. Your follow-up is a bridge to the next conversation, not the conversation itself. If you're writing more than 5-6 short paragraphs, you're writing a proposal, not a follow-up.

Can AI write follow-up emails that sound genuine?

Yes, if the AI has context from the actual conversation. Generic AI-written emails are obvious and counterproductive. But AI that pulls specific details from your meeting transcript produces follow-ups that are more specific than what most reps write manually. The key is human review: let the AI draft, then adjust the tone to sound like you.

When should you call instead of emailing?

When the deal is high-value (above your average deal size), when there's urgency (competitor evaluation, budget deadline), or when the buyer explicitly prefers phone. For everything else, email gives the buyer space to respond on their schedule, which most people prefer.
