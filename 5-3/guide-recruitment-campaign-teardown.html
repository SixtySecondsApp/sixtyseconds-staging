4,300 Leads in 2 Weeks: How We Did It

Chapter 1

The Context

The brief was three words: "As many as possible."

Manpower, one of the world's largest recruitment companies, needed drivers, warehouse operatives, and HGV drivers for their end-of-year PEAK campaign. The seasonal hiring window was narrow. The ad spend budget was £5,000. The expectation was volume.

Manpower runs PEAK campaigns every year to staff up for the Q4 logistics surge: Christmas deliveries, warehouse throughput, seasonal transport demand. They need thousands of people, fast, across multiple locations.

The challenge: everyone in recruitment is hiring for the same roles at the same time. Drivers and warehouse ops are the most competitive candidate markets in the UK during Q4. The big job boards charge premium rates. The candidates have options. And the window to reach them before a competitor does is measured in days, not weeks.

Previous campaigns had delivered decent results at typical recruitment marketing costs: £5–15 per application depending on the channel and the role. At £10 per application, a £5K ad spend gets you 500 applicants. Respectable. Not transformational.

We needed to break that economics entirely.

The Brief

Narrow seasonal window. Brief: as many drivers and warehouse operatives as possible. Two weeks later, we'd generated over 4,300 leads at £1.13 each - and the client told us to pause because the internal team couldn't process the volume fast enough.

Chapter 2

What We Actually Did

Five decisions drove the results. None of them were obvious at the time. All of them hold up as principles today.

We Ignored the Job Boards

This was the counterintuitive decision that changed everything. The default recruitment marketing playbook in the UK is: post the job on Indeed, Reed, Totaljobs, maybe run some sponsored listings, wait for applications. The problem: every other recruiter is doing the same thing. You're competing for attention on platforms where your ad looks identical to 200 others.

Instead, we went to where the candidates already were, but where nobody was running recruitment ads: Facebook and Instagram. In the mid-2010s, social media recruitment was still considered unconventional for blue-collar roles. Most agencies assumed warehouse operatives and drivers weren't on social media, or wouldn't respond to job ads there.

That assumption was wrong. These candidates were scrolling Facebook every evening after shifts. They just weren't scrolling Indeed.

We Made the Creative Feel Like Content, Not an Advert

Recruitment ads on social media in that era were terrible. A stock photo of someone in a hard hat. A list of requirements. A "click to apply" button. All signal, no story.

We built creative that looked like the content people were already engaging with. Short, punchy video and image ads that led with the benefit - seasonal pay, flexible shifts, local work - not the requirements. The tone was conversational, not corporate. The visuals were real, not stock.

The principle: If your ad looks like an ad, people scroll past it. If it looks like something a mate might share, they stop.

We Targeted Tight and Retargeted Tighter

Broad targeting wastes budget. We built audiences around location radius (people who lived within commuting distance of the specific depots and warehouses), job title and industry signals (people who'd listed relevant roles or worked in logistics, driving, warehouse), and behavioural signals (people who'd engaged with similar job content or competitor pages).

Then we layered retargeting hard. Anyone who watched 50% of a video ad, clicked through to the landing page, or started an application but didn't finish got a second and third touchpoint within 48 hours.

Most recruitment campaigns run one ad to a broad audience and hope. We ran multiple creatives to tight audiences and followed up with anyone who showed interest. The retargeting spend was roughly 30% of the total budget, and it generated over 40% of the leads.

We Removed Every Barrier From the Application Process

The typical recruitment application form in 2016–2018 asked for: full name, email, phone, address, work history, references, right-to-work documentation, availability, and sometimes a cover letter. That's a 15–20 minute process on a phone screen.

We stripped it to: name, phone number, and location. Three fields. Apply in under 60 seconds.

The recruiter could qualify candidates on the phone. The form's job was to capture intent, not collect data. Every additional field is a drop-off point. We removed all of them except the three the recruiter actually needed to make a call.

The Impact

Application completion rate went from the industry average of around 15–20% to over 60%.

We Optimised in Real-Time, Not Weekly

Most agency campaigns get reviewed weekly. We reviewed performance every few hours for the first 3 days, then daily after that.

We killed underperforming creatives within 24 hours. We shifted budget from broad audiences to the retargeting pools that were converting. We tested 8 creative variations in the first week and scaled the 3 that performed.

By day 4, we knew exactly which creative, audience, and placement combination was driving the cheapest leads. By day 7, we'd reallocated 70% of the budget to that combination. The second week was pure scaling of what was already proven.

Chapter 4

The 5 Principles

You don't need to be running recruitment campaigns to use what made this work. These principles apply to any high-volume marketing or sales motion.

Go where the competition isn't

Everyone was on job boards. We went to social. The candidates were in both places, but only one place had competition for their attention. Find the channel where your audience exists but your competitors haven't arrived yet.

Make it look like content, not marketing

Ads that look like ads get ignored. Content that happens to have a call to action gets engaged with. This applies to cold outreach, follow-up emails, LinkedIn posts, landing pages. If it reads like marketing, people's filters kick in.

Remove friction ruthlessly

Every step in a process is a drop-off point. We cut the application from 15 fields to 3 and tripled the completion rate. Ask yourself: what's the minimum information I need to start a conversation? Collect that. Nothing more.

Follow up with anyone who shows interest

40% of our leads came from retargeting. The first ad created awareness. The second and third ads converted it. Most campaigns run one touchpoint and call it done. The follow-up is where the ROI lives.

Optimise in hours, not weeks

Weekly reporting cycles mean you're spending money on things that stopped working 5 days ago. Check performance daily at minimum. Kill what's not working fast. Scale what is. Speed of iteration beats quality of planning.

Chapter 5

What This Taught Us About Building 60

This campaign was one of hundreds we ran at Sixty Seconds over 12 years. Different clients, different industries, different channels. But the same pattern kept appearing: the campaigns that worked were fast, specific, and systematic. They responded to signals quickly. They personalised based on real context. They followed up relentlessly. They removed admin friction so the team could focus on the work that mattered.

The campaigns that failed were slow, generic, and dependent on someone remembering to do the next step.

That pattern is exactly why we built 60.

60 is the product version of what our agency team did manually for hundreds of clients. The principles are identical:

  • Speed: 60 sends the follow-up within minutes of a meeting ending, not days. Because timing matters more than perfection.
  • Specificity: 60 pulls exact details from meeting transcripts so every follow-up references what the buyer actually said. Because generic outreach gets ignored.
  • Systematic follow-up: 60 builds cadences and tracks touchpoints so nothing falls through the cracks. Because the follow-up is where the ROI lives.
  • Friction removal: 60 compresses 40–70 minutes of post-meeting admin into under 10 minutes. Because every minute spent on admin is a minute not spent selling.

The Manpower campaign worked because we applied these principles manually with a talented team. 60 exists because those principles shouldn't require a team. They should be built into the system.
